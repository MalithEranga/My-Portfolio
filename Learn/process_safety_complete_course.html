LESSON 01

Why process safety matters

Safety culture, prevention, and high-consequence hazards

Process safety protects people, the environment and facilities from catastrophic releases of hazardous energy or materials. It is distinct from—but complementary to—personal or occupational safety.

Course learning pathway

The UC Davis course progresses from process safety culture to identifying hazards, evaluating risk, computing scenario frequencies, and selecting inherently safer designs. The primary goal is to prevent major accidents, not merely respond to them.

01Commit

Safety values, leadership, competence and openness.

02Understand

Hazards, scenarios, likelihood and severity.

03Manage

Prevention, mitigation, design and controls.

04Learn

Incidents, near misses, audits and improvement.

Eight features of a positive safety culture

  1. Safety as a core value: reinforce safety at the beginning of meetings through short “safety moments” and daily decisions.
  2. Committed leadership: management actively models safe behavior, assigns accountability and provides time, staff and money.
  3. Document and evaluate: record procedures, events, near misses and leading/lagging indicators; review performance rather than assuming silence means safety.
  4. A sense of vulnerability: maintain awareness that serious events remain possible even after long accident-free periods.
  5. Deference to expertise: empower technically knowledgeable people, including operators and maintenance staff, to influence decisions.
  6. Effective communication: transfer accurate risk information across teams and shifts and encourage candid reporting.
  7. Mutual trust: distinguish honest mistakes from reckless choices and avoid retaliation for raising concerns; support stop-work authority.
  8. Timely response: investigate concerns and close corrective actions promptly with evidence of completion.

Process safety versus occupational safety

Process safetyOccupational / personal safety
Loss of containment, fires, explosions, toxic releases, process upsetsSlips, trips, falls, ergonomics, isolated injuries
Frequently low-frequency / high-consequenceOften more frequent, localized events (though some can be severe)
Focus: plant systems, process integrity, barriers and accident scenariosFocus: direct exposure and individual working practices
A low personal injury rate does not demonstrate good major-accident preventionMetrics may not detect latent process hazards

Helpful references mentioned during the course

  • Chemical Process Safety: Fundamentals with Applications — Daniel A. Crowl and Joseph F. Louvar.
  • What Went Wrong? Case Histories of Process Plant Disasters and How They Could Have Been Avoided — Trevor Kletz and Paul Amyotte.
  • Introduction to Process Safety for Undergraduates and Engineers — CCPS.

Safety culture is a system, not a slogan

Organizations must deliberately combine leadership, reporting, hazard awareness, controls and continuous learning.

LESSON 03

Bhopal and inherently safer choices

1984 methyl isocyanate disaster — lessons from a catastrophe

The 1984 Bhopal disaster involved a major toxic release of methyl isocyanate (MIC). It showed how poor maintenance, inadequate risk understanding, and weak emergency readiness can combine into a catastrophe.

☣
CASE STUDY • 1984 • BHOPAL, INDIA

When a hazardous inventory becomes a community hazard

Risk extends beyond workers to surrounding residents, who may be exposed involuntarily and without the benefit or knowledge of plant operations.

Course-discussed risk factors

  • Large storage inventory of highly hazardous intermediate MIC.
  • Maintenance and protective systems that were not adequately reliable.
  • Insufficient identification and evaluation of major release scenarios.
  • Inadequate emergency communication and preparation for off-site consequences.

Inherently safer alternative

Rather than maintaining a large MIC intermediate inventory, consider producing smaller amounts as needed and consuming them promptly. This is the Minimize principle of inherently safer design. It does not remove all hazards, but directly reduces the amount available for release.

✦
Bhopal lesson

Reducing the hazardous inventory at source can be more robust than relying exclusively on layers of protection.

LESSON 04

Alcoa, Paul O’Neill and leadership

How visibly prioritizing worker safety changed an organization

When Paul O’Neill became CEO of Alcoa in 1987, he made worker safety a highly visible strategic priority. The course used this as a culture and leadership example.

What the case illustrates

  • Investors initially questioned the emphasis on safety instead of conventional financial targets.
  • Leadership openly committed to preventing workplace injury, including an aspiration of zero injuries.
  • After a worker death, leadership emphasized learning and organizational responsibility, not superficial blame.
  • Clearer incident reporting and rapid response helped identify operational weaknesses.
  • Improvements in processes and communication could reinforce both safer working and overall performance.

Practical safety behavior at home, university and industry

Before work: read the SDS, label containers, understand equipment limits and know emergency routes. During work: use suitable PPE, engineering controls, good housekeeping and stop work when conditions change. Afterwards: report near misses, maintain equipment, store materials safely and update procedures.

✦
Leadership sets the tone

Good safety outcomes require systems that listen to concerns and close identified gaps, not just motivational statements.

LESSON 05

Identify hazards and understand risk

Probability, severity, tolerability and ethical risk distinctions

Risk is concerned with how likely a harmful event is and how serious the harm would be. Hazard is the inherent capacity to cause harm; risk depends on the scenario and exposure.

Risk ∝ Severity × Likelihood (or event frequency)

The simple product is useful for teaching and some matrices, but full quantitative assessment requires consistent units, a well-defined endpoint, and uncertainty treatment.

Voluntary vs involuntary risk

Risk typeDefinitionIllustrations
VoluntaryA person knowingly accepts a hazard in exchange for a perceived benefitHazardous sport; some activities or occupational choices
InvoluntaryOthers face the hazard without direct benefit, genuine knowledge, or meaningful choiceResidents near chemical plants; exposure to secondhand smoke

Historical frequency comparisons from the lecture

The course used historical U.S. fatality data (including 2014 injury causes and 2003–2018 workplace fatalities), illustrating how risks differ by activity and how data must be normalized before comparison.

  • U.S. annual workplace fatalities in the example were generally around 5,000–6,000; 2018 ≈ 5,250, equivalent to about 14.4 deaths per day.
  • Equivalent worker exposure in the lecture: 40 hours/week × 50 weeks/year = 2,000 hours per FTE-year.
  • For 100,000 full-time-equivalent workers, the denominator is 200 million hours/year.
  • Figures for poisoning and motor vehicle fatalities were on the rough order of 10 per 100,000 in the example, while drowning was on the order of 1; rarer causes included floods, lightning and extreme temperatures.
  • Chemical manufacturing had lower reported occupational fatality rates than some sectors such as agriculture and construction in the lecture comparison, but this alone does not assess major accident hazards.

These numbers are historical lecture examples, not contemporary official rates.

LESSON 07

Select the right hazard analysis

Checklist, HAZOP, FTA, ETA and LOPA compared

Hazard identification asks what can go wrong; consequence evaluation asks what happens next; risk evaluation asks how often and how serious; risk management asks what to change.

TechniqueNatureStrongest use
Checklist analysisNon-scenario, systematic audit against expert questionsKnown/standard facilities and established operating practices
HAZOPGuideword-led, scenario-based team studyComplex process deviations, interactions and new designs
Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)Deductive, top event to basic causesLogical combinations that create a specific failure
Event Tree Analysis (ETA)Inductive, initiating event to outcomesHow downstream safety functions influence consequences
LOPASimplified semi-quantitative safeguards reviewWhether selected independent layers meet a risk target

Seven selection considerations from the course

  1. Motivation: regulatory requirement or internal corporate need.
  2. Process phase: conceptual design, pilot, commissioned operation or modification.
  3. Required outputs: qualitative findings, recommendations or numerical frequency estimates.
  4. Available information: P&IDs, equipment specifications, SDS and failure data.
  5. Complexity and hazard nature: interacting controls, process chemistry and operating modes.
  6. Perceived risk: severity and possible accident scenarios.
  7. People and time: expert availability, resources and review depth.

Checklist method

  1. Develop or select a checklist suitable for the process.
  2. Review with people who know the facility.
  3. Record outcomes: completed / not applicable / further work required.
  4. Assign, track and close corrective actions.

Course checklist topics

Process (20 questions in the referenced textbook section): SDS availability, chemistry understood, inherently safer options considered, utility failures evaluated.

Equipment (12 questions): recognized design codes, appropriate corrosion allowance and labeling.

Safety equipment: fire protection, available water supplies and detection/gas-sampling arrangements.

Only representative checklist items were supplied in the course transcript. The complete original 20 + 12 individual question texts were not available and are not invented here.

Choosing between checklist and HAZOP — practice dialogue

Standard bulk benzene tank with two decades of experience: a checklist may effectively screen recognized hazards, provided the scope and risk justify that depth. Novel continuous-flow specialty chemical reactor with heating, cooling and catalyst injection: a team HAZOP is more appropriate for unfamiliar interacting deviations.

✦
Selection is risk-based

More complicated analysis is not automatically better; choose a method that can answer the actual safety question.

LESSON 08

HAZOP — systematic process deviations

Nodes, parameters, guide words, causes, safeguards and recommendations

HAZOP (Hazard and Operability Study) is a structured multidisciplinary study of deviations from design intent.

Essential documents

  • Process flow diagrams (PFDs), piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs).
  • Operating philosophy, process chemistry, SDS, mass and energy balances.
  • Materials of construction, equipment ratings, instrumentation and existing protection.

Walk-through methodology

  1. Break the process into nodes (a vessel, heat exchanger, line or logical process segment).
  2. State design intent and choose process parameter, e.g. flow, pressure, temperature, composition.
  3. Apply a suitable guideword to form a meaningful deviation such as No Flow or More Temperature.
  4. Identify credible causes, consequences and existing safeguards.
  5. Propose actions and assign responsibility.
  6. Repeat for other deviations and nodes and verify follow-up closure.
Guide wordMeaningIllustrative deviation
No / None / NotTotal absence of intended conditionNo Flow
MoreQuantitative increaseMore Temperature / More Pressure
LessQuantitative decreaseLess Flow
As Well AsUnwanted additional constituent or activityAs Well As: impurity in feed
Part OfOnly part of intended content or functionPart Of: missing toluene component
ReverseOpposite directionReverse Flow / backflow
Other ThanDifferent from design intentOther Than: unintended reaction

Only apply meaningful word–parameter combinations; “No Temperature,” for example, is generally not a useful process deviation.

✦
HAZOP discipline

Each recommendation should address a clearly documented cause–deviation–consequence sequence, with credit given only to verified safeguards.

LESSON 09

Piper Alpha and Williams Geismar

Barriers, isolation, relief and management of change

⚙
CASE STUDY • PIPER ALPHA • 1988

167 fatalities in an offshore platform catastrophe

A series of permit, handover, design and emergency protection failures compounded a major hydrocarbon event.

Piper Alpha — course-discussed chain

  1. A pump was out of service for maintenance; a safety/relief component was removed and the equipment was unsafe to restart.
  2. Permit-to-work documentation and shift handover failed to communicate that isolation status adequately.
  3. Another pump failed and the isolated pump was started, contributing to a hydrocarbon release.
  4. Gas handling changes increased hazardous inventory and escalation potential without sufficiently reassessing the installation.
  5. Firewater pumps had been left in manual mode during diving operations, impairing automatic emergency response.
  6. Fire and explosion escalation overwhelmed the facility.

Key safeguards: reliable PTW / lockout-tagout / isolation, face-to-face shift handover, proper management of change (MOC), emergency firewater logic, and systematic review of layout and hydrocarbon inventories.

♨
CASE STUDY • WILLIAMS GEISMAR • 2013

2 fatalities and 167 injuries

Trapped liquid propane heated within isolated equipment until mechanical failure occurred.

Williams Geismar olefins plant — hazard mechanism

  • A standby reboiler contained liquid propane trapped between isolation boundaries.
  • Heating from hot water expanded the liquid, increasing pressure through thermal expansion.
  • The relief path was unavailable/isolated; the vessel ruptured, followed by explosion and fire.
  • Checklist review should include valve alignment, trapped liquid, isolation and thermal relief.
  • HAZOP deviations include More Pressure, More Temperature and No Flow.
  • PHA, MOC, suitable relief arrangements and mechanical integrity are important lessons.
✦
Do not evaluate safeguards in isolation

Human procedures, physical protection and process modifications must all be tested against credible failure paths.

LESSON 10

Probability, reliability and failure rates

Exponential failure model, bathtub curve, mean life and time windows

Reliability analysis starts with a clearly defined component failure event, a time horizon, and a failure-rate model. The course used a constant failure-rate approximation, represented here by μ (mu).

Three related functions

R(t) = e−μt    •    P(T ≤ t) = 1 − e−μt
f(t) = μe−μt    •    MTTF = 1/μ

R(t): probability of surviving without failure through time t. P(T≤t): probability of failure by t. f(t): failure-time probability density (not annual event frequency). MTTF: mean time to failure for this exponential, nonrepairable-component model.

For any interval:

P(t₁ < T ≤ t₂) = e−μt₁ − e−μt₂

Illustrative component rates from the lesson

ComponentApproximate failures/year (μ)
Control valve0.6
Hand-operated valve0.13
Level measurement1.7
Pressure relief device0.022
Thermocouple0.52

These numbers reproduce illustrative historical lecture data. They are not current equipment-specific SIL reliability data or appropriate for real design without qualification.

Bathtub failure rate curve

Infant mortality: early failures; useful life: roughly constant rate; wear-out: increasing failure rate. The constant μ assumption works best for the middle region.

MTBF versus MTTF

The lecture informally calls 1/μ a mean time between failures (MTBF). More precisely, MTTF = 1/μ for a nonrepairable exponential lifetime, while MTBF is commonly reserved for repairable items and may require separate operating/repair cycle definitions.

LESSON 11

Series and parallel systems

AND/OR reliability, redundancy and a mixed-system example

A system’s reliability depends on how failures combine. Independence is essential to the simple multiplication formulas.

Series success structure

All components must work; a failure in any one causes system failure.

Rseries = ∏ Rᵢ    •    Pseries fail = 1 − ∏(1 − Pᵢ)
For independent constant μᵢ:   μseries = Σ μᵢ

Parallel success structure

One functioning component is enough; all independent parallel channels must fail for the system to fail.

Pparallel fail = ∏ Pᵢ    •    Rparallel = 1 − ∏(1 − Rᵢ)

Worked mixed system: component 1 in series with (2 || 3)

At t = 1 year, failure rates are μ₁ = 0.29, μ₂ = 1.12, μ₃ = 0.52 per year:

R₁ = e−0.29 ≈ 0.748;   R₂ = e−1.12 ≈ 0.326;   R₃ = e−0.52 ≈ 0.595
Rsystem = R₁[1 − (1 − R₂)(1 − R₃)] ≈ 0.544
Psystem failure ≈ 0.456

The 1-year equivalent constant hazard, if represented as −ln(R)/t, is about 0.61/year. This equivalence is specific to the selected time: a series/parallel mixture generally does not have a time-invariant effective failure rate.

Other course quiz examples

  • Three independent series component reliabilities: 0.83 × 0.75 × 0.67 = 0.417 (approximately).
  • Three independent parallel component failure probabilities: 0.10 × 0.17 × 0.22 = 0.00374.
  • For μ = 0.5/year over 3 years: R(3)=e−1.5≈0.223.
✦
Redundancy needs independence

Common power, instrument air, environmental hazards, faulty procedures or software can defeat apparently separate channels.

LESSON 12

Availability and inspection

Revealed failures, unrevealed failures, proof-testing and downtime

Reliability predicts how long until failure. Availability is the fraction of time that a repairable system is usable when needed; a hidden failure may make an item unavailable until inspection.

Revealed failures

Failure is detected immediately or promptly, followed by repair. If the equipment operates for τo and needs τr for repair:

A = τo / (τo + τr)    •    U = τr / (τo + τr)

Course quiz: operating 45 days, repair 3 days.

A = 45 / (45 + 3) = 0.9375;   U = 3 / 48 = 0.0625

The lesson calls the 48-day operating-plus-repair cycle the MTBF under its simplified convention.

Unrevealed failures

Some safety components fail silently; periodic inspections discover a dormant failure. With exponentially distributed failures, inspection every τi, and negligible repair time:

Uavg = 1 − [1 − e−μτᵢ] / (μτᵢ)
Aavg = [1 − e−μτᵢ] / (μτᵢ)
Expected failed time per cycle = τᵢ − [1 − e−μτᵢ] / μ

This is an average unavailability over the inspection cycle. The lecture also offered a simpler timing approximation: if an item works about 50 days of a 61-day inspection interval and then stays undetected failed 11 days, approximate U ≈ 11/61 = 0.18. That specific 11-day estimate is a course scenario, not a universal property of periodic tests.

Inspection interval trade-off

Long intervals can leave safety-critical failures undetected. Shorter tests improve discovery, though intrusive testing can itself cause downtime or introduce errors; use equipment-specific maintenance strategies.

✦
Proof tests are part of barrier management

A safety system that looks present but is failed on demand cannot be credited as dependable protection.

LESSON 13

Event Tree Analysis (ETA)

Initiating frequencies, success/failure branches and scenario outcomes

Event trees work forward from a clearly defined initiating event through sequential safety functions to distinct outcomes. Each outcome has its own frequency.

Construction steps

  1. Identify the initiating event and frequency F₀.
  2. List safeguards/functions in the order in which they matter.
  3. For each relevant function, branch to success or failure; traditionally success branches upward and failure downward.
  4. Skip a safety function when it is not applicable to a particular path.
  5. Multiply the initiating frequency by relevant conditional branch probabilities to obtain path frequency.
  6. Confirm terminal frequencies sum to the initiating event frequency (for a complete, mutually exclusive tree).
Fsuccess = F₀(1 − p₁)
Fpartial = F₀p₁(1 − p₂)
Fboth fail = F₀p₁p₂

Reactor cooling failure example

Consider failure of cooling to a reactor; pressure may increase. Candidate functions in a simple teaching tree are high-pressure alarm → automatic shutdown → relief valve. A worst-case branch could involve failures of all relevant functions and culminate in vessel rupture/explosion. A real tree must reflect correct dependencies and whether each function still applies following prior successes.

COURSE STUDY NOTES / MODULE 03
LESSON 15

Deepwater Horizon: failure of barriers

2010 Macondo blowout, blind shear ram and common-cause failures

⚓
CASE STUDY • DEEPWATER HORIZON • 2010

11 workers killed; a devastating offshore oil release

This case connected fault trees, redundancy, hidden failures, physical process conditions and safeguard effectiveness.

Well control safety functions

  • Blowout Preventer (BOP): well-control equipment intended to close in or shear/seal the well during a critical event.
  • Annular preventer and pipe rams: different closure functions.
  • Blind Shear Ram (BSR): intended to sever drill pipe and close off the flow path.
  • Automatic Mode Function (AMF / deadman): intended to activate under loss of electrical/control/hydraulic connection.
  • Redundant control pods: blue and yellow pod channels contained electrical, computing and solenoid components.

Why redundancy did not guarantee success

According to the CSB account discussed in the course, a yellow control pod had an incorrectly wired solenoid and a battery issue; these deficiencies may have offset one another sufficiently for BSR actuation. Despite actuation, pressure-induced effective compression bent/buckled the drill pipe away from the shear blade center. The ram could not completely shear and seal the well. Thus commanded actuation was not the same as successful containment.

Do not assume a simple pair of parallel electrical pods completely represents the overall BOP. Mechanical geometry, pressure forces, maintenance, sensing, activation logic and common-cause conditions can dominate.

Practical fault-tree thinking

Top event: failed well sealing. Branches could include failure to command/actuate OR physical inability to shear/seal. The first category includes combinations of control-channel failures; the second includes pipe position and strength conditions.

✦
Success criteria must be physical

A safety system succeeds only if it prevents the defined undesired consequence, not merely if its actuator signal was sent.

LESSON 16

Constructing fault trees

Top events, logic gates, event types, boundaries and cut sets

Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) is deductive: start with an unwanted top event and work backwards through AND/OR gates to more basic causes.

FTA versus ETA

FTA — backward / deductiveETA — forward / inductive
Starts at a top event and asks what combinations cause itStarts at an initiating event and asks what follows
Represents failure combinations with AND/OR gatesRepresents safety function success/failure branches
Develops minimal cut setsDevelops terminal consequence frequencies

Fault tree symbols

EventRectangleTop/intermediate event
ECircleBasic event
?DiamondUndeveloped event
ANDAND gateAll inputs required
OROR gateOne or more inputs

An external event represents a boundary condition or event external to the selected fault tree, whereas an inhibit gate requires the input event and a qualifying condition. A basic event is deliberately not divided further at the chosen resolution; an undeveloped event is not detailed because suitable information is missing.

Minimal cut set

The smallest sufficient set of basic failures leading to the top event. Example:

Top = B₁ AND (B₂ OR B₃ OR B₄)
Minimal cut sets: {B₁,B₂}, {B₁,B₃}, {B₁,B₄}

Every pathway requires B₁, together with one of B₂/B₃/B₄. Shorter cut sets often warrant attention because fewer independent things need to go wrong, but actual likelihood depends on event probabilities, exposure and dependencies.

Analysis boundaries and resolution

  • Boundaries: decide whether rare external hazards such as earthquakes, tornadoes or hurricanes are credible for this geographic site and study objective.
  • Resolution: treat a valve failure as a basic event or further decompose it into actuator, stem, seal, instrument air and signal failures.
  • Computerization: Boolean logic and cut-set enumeration scale well with software, but a result is only as good as the assumptions and input data.

Probability of independent events

P(A AND B) = P(A) × P(B)
P(A OR B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A)P(B)
✦
Specify the top event unambiguously

“Tank overpressure” and “tank rupture with off-site harm” are different events and yield different trees and protection credit.

LESSON 18

Bow-tie diagrams

Connecting fault trees to event trees and separating barrier types

A bow-tie shows an entire incident scenario from threats → central event → consequences. Its left side resembles a fault tree and its right side resembles an event tree.

Construction

  1. Define the hazardous central/top event precisely (often loss of containment).
  2. Map credible threat causes on the left, including preventive safeguards.
  3. Map feasible consequence paths and mitigating safety functions on the right.
  4. Use fault tree logic and event tree frequencies when quantitative estimation is needed.
  5. Inspect barrier independence, effectiveness, escalation mechanisms and responsibilities.

Example: tank overpressurization

Left-hand threats: blocked outlet, thermal heating, external fire, control valve failure, overfilling where liquid expansion occurs. Preventive measures: process limitations, independent pressure trip, suitable relief before rupture. Right-hand outcomes: safe pressure management, vessel rupture and chemical release, potentially fire/toxic exposure. Mitigation: dikes, fire suppression, blast separation and emergency response.

Control valve sub-fault tree: actuator defect OR loss of instrument air/power OR bad control signal OR mechanical seizure. A detailed tree can use these as elementary faults; a higher-level study may treat the overall valve failure as a basic event.

✦
A bow-tie is a communication tool

It shows who and what protects the process, while FTA and ETA quantify causes and outcomes when needed.

LESSON 19

Independent Protection Layers (IPLs)

Eight common protections, six criteria and safeguards credit

A protection layer is not automatically an Independent Protection Layer (IPL). LOPA credits a barrier only when it is demonstrably capable of the risk reduction claimed for the specific scenario.

Examples of protection layers

LayerIllustrationTypical role
1. Process designInherently safer selection or designReduce the inherent hazard
2. Basic process control systemNormal reactor temperature loopMaintain normal safe operation
3. Critical alarm + interventionHigh-pressure alarm with trained operatorRespond to abnormal condition
4. Safety instrumented functionIndependent emergency shutdownAutomatically move to a safe state
5. Physical protectionRelief devicePrevent catastrophic equipment failure
6. Post-release physical protectionDike/bundConfine spilled liquid
7. Plant emergency responseOn-site respondersLimit escalation/exposure
8. Community emergency responseExternal responders and alertsProtect community after escalation

Six IPL qualification criteria

1Independence

Not defeated by the initiating cause or by failure of another credited IPL.

2Functionality

Actually provides the intended protection for the specified hazardous scenario.

3Reliability

Operates on demand under stated conditions and over the assigned period.

4Auditability

Its condition, testing and performance can be demonstrated and reviewed.

5Access security

Management prevents unauthorized or uncontrolled changes.

6Management of change

Modifications are reviewed, approved and documented.

Alarm, procedure and pressure relief valve

In the course dialogue, a pressure relief device was presented as the stronger IPL candidate because it can be independent of normal control and human reaction. A written procedure is generally not a strong independent barrier by itself; alarm-plus-operator-response can sometimes receive IPL credit when sufficient independence, response time, training, validation and PFD evidence exist. Merely labeling a device “automatic” does not establish IPL eligibility.

Active versus passive safeguards

Active: needs to detect/respond or move (e.g. opens a relief valve). Passive: remains protective without an active response (e.g. sufficiently strong wall, dike). Neither automatically equals inherently safer design: a thicker wall primarily resists failure, whereas eliminating a high-pressure requirement changes the hazard at its source.

✦
Challenge shared dependencies

A trip and normal controller connected to the same sensor or instrument air supply may fail together and cannot casually be counted as two independent IPLs.

LESSON 20

LOPA method and risk tolerability

Scenario definition, PFD, conditional modifiers and limitations

Layer of Protection Analysis (LOPA) is a semi-quantitative, order-of-magnitude technique to decide whether identified independent protection layers reduce the frequency of a specified harmful cause–consequence scenario sufficiently.

Step-by-step workflow

  1. Choose one cause–consequence pair found using HAZOP or other hazard study.
  2. Set initiating event frequency with well-defined boundaries and any failures assumed to have already occurred.
  3. Account for enabling conditions, process on-stream demand, occupancy/exposure and applicable conditional modifiers without double-counting.
  4. Identify IPLs that meet all qualification criteria.
  5. Assign PFD (probability of failure on demand) to each credited IPL, using defensible data.
  6. Calculate mitigated consequence frequency and compare with target.
  7. Improve the design/barriers and recalculate where the risk is not tolerable.
fmitigated = finitiating, adjusted × ∏ PFDIPL,j
Risk reduction factor (RRF) = 1 / ∏PFDIPL,j

Units: initiating and mitigated frequencies are events/year. A PFD is a dimensionless probability (e.g. 0.01), not “PFD/year”.

LOPA limitations

  • Not a hazard-discovery technique: needs an already identified scenario.
  • Approximate resolution: relies on orders of magnitude; complex interdependent scenarios may require detailed quantitative risk assessment.
  • Data and tolerability criteria vary: cross-organization comparisons may not be meaningful if assumptions and criteria differ.
  • Conditional independence: incorrectly assuming independence overstates risk reduction.
  • Risk target context: severity, population exposure and site obligations matter.

F–N diagrams

The lecture connected consequence and frequency tolerability boundaries. In standard societal risk terminology, an F–N curve commonly shows frequency F of events causing N or more fatalities versus N. Course visual descriptions also refer generally to a consequence–frequency acceptability region; those are related but not identical displays.

Hazardous material loss of containment — dike and response

The lecture considers an atmospheric tank of hazardous material failing with a consequence equivalent to 10× the chemical threshold quantity (TQ). Operating demand is 100%; available safeguards are a dike and human response within 40 minutes.

Calculation

fadjusted = 10−3 × 1 = 10−3 events/year
fmitigated = 10−3 × 10−2 × 10−1 = 10−6 events/year
10−6 < 10−5 ⇒ target achieved
RRF = 10−3 / 10−6 = 1000

Lecture conclusion: 0 additional IPLs are needed to meet this specific scenario target, provided the protection layers perform as assumed.

Careful interpretation: a dike and emergency response mitigate consequences after a tank has lost containment. They do not reduce the frequency of the initial tank failure itself; the calculated 10⁻⁶/year represents the designated severe consequence endpoint, conditional on the defined scenario.

LESSON 22

Four principles of inherently safer design

Minimize, substitute, moderate and simplify

Inherently Safer Design (ISD) reduces hazards by changing the fundamental process, rather than relying only on devices, procedures or emergency responses.

01

Minimize

Use smaller quantities of hazardous chemicals, reduce material holdup, inventory and intermediates.

Example:

Replace a large batch reactor with a smaller continuous reactor when process design supports it. Make reactive/toxic intermediates close to their point of consumption.

02

Substitute

Replace hazardous materials, equipment or chemistry with safer alternatives.

Example:

Use a less hazardous solvent; consider water instead of hot oil for heat transfer when the duty, corrosion and design permit.

03

Moderate

Use less hazardous operating conditions or material forms.

Example:

Lower operating temperature and pressure, or select reactor conditions with less potential for runaway reaction.

04

Simplify

Remove avoidable equipment, instrumentation and operational complexity.

Example:

Improve control displays, simplify procedures and layouts, reduce piping length and select maintainable equipment.

Hierarchy of controls

Elimination / inherently safer hazard removal
Substitution and lower-hazard conditions
Engineering controls and safeguards
Administrative controls and safe work procedures
Personal protective equipment (PPE)

The lesson emphasizes that hazard removal and substitution are more robust than merely controlling the consequences. PPE is essential where exposure remains, but is generally the least effective barrier if used alone. This five-tier view expands the lecture’s simplified comparison of ISD, engineering controls and PPE.

Active and passive protection versus inherent safety

Active relief valve: must actuate when required. Passive stronger tank wall: provides containment without responding. ISD redesign: use a genuinely compatible atmospheric-pressure chemical to remove the high-pressure storage hazard. The alternative must be assessed for its own toxicity, flammability, reactivity and environmental impact.

✦
Best-first strategy

Consider elimination, inventory reduction and substitution before relying on more elaborate protective layers; continue to manage residual risk.

Everyday application: a bicycle commute

Four ISD principles applied to a 2-mile trip

The second ISD video applied inherently safer design beyond industry. Its teaching example was a two-mile bicycle commute to UC Davis.

PrincipleBicycle commute design ideaRisk-reduction rationale
MinimizeMove closer so the trip is shorterLess exposure time or distance
SubstituteSwitch to a safer mode such as a bus, where appropriateReplace the risky activity
ModerateUse a separated bike path or route with fewer carsLess hazardous surrounding conditions
SimplifyChoose fewer complicated left turns across trafficFewer decision/conflict points

Apply the approach to a university laboratory

PrincipleLab example
MinimizeUse microscale samples and the smallest practicable hazardous reagent inventory
SubstituteChoose a less hazardous solvent or method if analytically suitable
ModerateUse lower reaction temperature and pressure and dilute solutions where possible
SimplifyUse clear labels, fewer transfers, unambiguous setup and easy-to-follow controls

Decision dialogue: add IPLs or redesign?

Proposal A adds an independent pressure relief valve and a high-pressure alarm. Proposal B requires a higher initial investment to substitute a compatible material that can be stored at atmospheric pressure, removing the particular overpressure mechanism. Preference: favor B where verified to avoid new hazards; compare lifecycle economics, feasibility, chemical compatibility, energy use, maintenance, residual accident likelihood, environmental effects and MOC. Proposal A still may be necessary for remaining threats.
