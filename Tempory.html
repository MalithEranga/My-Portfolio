UNIT 01

INTRODUCTION TO ATOMS AND MOLECULES

1.1 What is Chemistry?

Chemistry is the branch of science that studies matter, its composition, structure, properties, and the changes that matter undergoes. Chemistry explains:

  • What substances are made of.
  • How atoms combine to form molecules and compounds.
  • Why chemical reactions occur.
  • How energy changes during chemical reactions.
  • How the arrangement of electrons affects chemical properties.
  • How chemical reactions are used in laboratories, industries, medicine, and environmental protection.

1.3 Classification of Matter

Matter can be divided into pure substances and mixtures.

Pure Substances (Fixed Composition)

• Elements: Contain only one type of chemical element.
• Compounds: Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.

Mixtures (Physically Combined)

• Homogeneous: Uniform composition (e.g., saltwater).
• Heterogeneous: Non-uniform composition (e.g., sand mixed with water).

1.2 Matter & States of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Examples: water, oxygen, iron, carbon dioxide, and sodium chloride. Matter exists mainly in three familiar physical states: solid, liquid, and gas. Plasma is another important state.

1.4 Atom & 1.5 Molecule

Atom Definition: An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element that retains the chemical identity of that element. An atom consists of:

  1. A central nucleus containing protons and usually neutrons.
  2. Electrons occupying quantum states in the region surrounding the nucleus.

Most of the volume of an atom is occupied by its electron cloud. Examples of elements are H, O, Na, Mg, Cl, and Fe.

Molecule Definition: A molecule is an electrically neutral group of two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds and acting as a distinct unit.

• Diatomic molecule: Contains two atoms (O₂, H₂, N₂, HCl).
• Polyatomic molecule: Contains more than two atoms (H₂O, NH₃, CH₄).
• Not every compound consists of separate molecules. Sodium chloride, for example, forms an extended ionic crystal lattice.

1.7 Atomic Number & Mass Number

The general nuclear symbol is:

\[ {}^{A}_{Z}X \quad \implies \quad Z = \text{Protons}, \quad A = \text{Protons} + \text{Neutrons} \] \[ \boxed{\text{Neutrons} = A - Z} \]

For a neutral atom, \(\text{Electrons} = Z\). For an ion, the electron number changes according to the ionic charge.

Worked Example 1: Number of Subatomic Particles

Find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in \({}^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\).

• Step 1 — Protons: \(p = 13\)

• Step 2 — Neutrons: \(n = 27 - 13 = 14\)

• Step 3 — Electrons: The \(3+\) ion has lost three electrons: \(e = 13 - 3 = 10\)

Answer: 13 protons, 14 neutrons, and 10 electrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Chemical properties are often very similar because isotopes have the same electronic configuration.

Chlorine contains 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37.

1.9 Mole Concept

One mole is the amount of substance containing exactly \(6.02214076\times10^{23}\) specified elementary entities (Avogadro constant \(N_A\)).

\[ N_A = 6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} \] \[ n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad N = nN_A \]

Worked Example 3: Water Molecules

Find the number of water molecules in 9.0 g of water (\(M \approx 18.0\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)).

\[ n = \frac{9.0}{18.0} = 0.50\ \mathrm{mol} \]

\[ N = 0.50(6.022\times10^{23}) = \boxed{3.011\times10^{23}\text{ molecules}} \]

2.2 Discovery of the Electron (J. J. Thomson)

Thomson studied cathode rays using a discharge tube. Important observations:

  1. Cathode rays travelled from the negative electrode toward the positive electrode.
  2. They were deflected by electric and magnetic fields.
  3. Their behavior showed that they contained negatively charged particles.
  4. Similar charge-to-mass ratios were obtained using different electrode materials.
\[ \left|\frac{e}{m_e}\right| \approx 1.759\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}} \]

2.3 Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment

Robert Millikan measured the elementary electric charge by observing charged oil droplets in an electric field:

\[ e = 1.602176634\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C} \quad \implies \quad m_e = \frac{e}{|e/m_e|} \]

2.4 Discovery of the Atomic Nucleus (Rutherford)

Rutherford's gold foil experiment involved directing alpha particles toward very thin gold foil.

Observations:

  • Most alpha particles passed through the foil.
  • Some were deflected; a very small number experienced large deflections.

Conclusions (Disproved Plum Pudding Model):

  1. Most atomic volume is relatively empty.
  2. Most atomic mass is concentrated in a small nucleus.
  3. The nucleus has a positive electric charge.
  4. Electrons occupy the region around the nucleus.

2.5 Neutron, 2.6 Stability & 2.7 Ions

2.5 Neutron: James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. It has no net electrical charge and a mass slightly greater than a proton, explaining why atoms of the same element can have different mass numbers.

2.6 Nuclear Stability: The strong nuclear interaction holds protons and neutrons together at very short distances against electrostatic repulsion. Stability depends on the proton-to-neutron ratio and nuclear binding energy. Unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay.

2.7 Ions & Electron Transfer: The nucleus does not change during ordinary chemical ion formation.

\[ \mathrm{Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-} \quad (\text{Cation}) \] \[ \mathrm{Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-} \quad (\text{Anion}) \]
UNIT 03

DEVELOPMENT OF ATOMIC THEORY

3.1 Dalton's Atomic Theory & 3.2 Classical Laws

  1. Matter consists of atoms.
  2. Atoms of a particular element were considered identical.
  3. Atoms of different elements have different properties.
  4. Compounds form when atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.
  5. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms.

Limitations: Atoms contain subatomic particles; isotopes have different masses; atoms can change in nuclear reactions; some solids have non-molecular extended lattices.

Law of Conservation of Mass

In a closed system, total mass of reactants equals total mass of products: \(2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O\).

Law of Definite Proportions

A pure chemical compound contains its constituent elements in a fixed proportion by mass (e.g., H₂O always has H:O mass ratio ≈ 1:8).

Law of Multiple Proportions

CO has 16 g O per 12 g C; CO₂ has 32 g O per 12 g C (1:2 small whole-number ratio).

3.3 Thomson, 3.4 Rutherford & 3.5 Bohr's Atomic Model

• 3.3 Thomson's Model: Negative electrons embedded in a positively charged sphere (plum pudding). Failed to explain alpha scattering.
• 3.4 Rutherford's Model: Dense positive nucleus surrounded by electrons. Classical physics predicted orbiting electrons should radiate energy and spiral inward; couldn't explain discrete emission spectra.

3.5 Bohr's Postulates & Energy Formula

  1. Electrons occupy permitted stationary states without radiating energy continuously.
  2. Energy is emitted or absorbed during transitions between allowed states (\(\Delta E = E_{\mathrm{final}} - E_{\mathrm{initial}}\)).
\[ E_n = -\frac{2.1799\times10^{-18}Z^2}{n^2}\ \mathrm{J} \]

Worked Example 4: Energy of Hydrogen Electron (n = 3)

\[ E_3 = -\frac{2.1799\times10^{-18}}{3^2} = -\frac{2.1799\times10^{-18}}{9} = \boxed{-2.422\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}} \]

3.6 Limitations of Bohr's Model: Only works for 1-electron species; fails for multi-electron spectra, fine structure, line intensities, and uses classical definite paths instead of quantum wavefunctions.

UNIT 04

QUANTUM THEORY AND ATOMIC ORBITALS

4.1–4.3 Electromagnetic Radiation & Planck's Quantum Theory

Quantum theory explains discrete atomic energy levels, atomic spectra, electron wave behavior, orbital shapes, and electron configurations. Electromagnetic radiation (radio, microwave, IR, visible, UV, X-ray, gamma) travels in vacuum at the speed of light:

\[ \boxed{c = \lambda\nu} \quad (c = 2.99792458\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}) \] \[ \boxed{E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}} \quad (h = 6.62607015\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}) \]

Worked Example 5: Photon Energy (λ = 500 nm)

Convert to metres: \(500\ \mathrm{nm} = 5.00\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}\)

\[ E = \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)}{5.00\times10^{-7}} = \boxed{3.98\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}} \]

Energy per mole: \(E_{\mathrm{mol}} = E N_A \approx \boxed{240\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}\)

4.4–4.6 De Broglie Duality & Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

\[ \text{De Broglie: } \boxed{\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}} \qquad \text{Heisenberg: } \boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}} \implies \boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta v_x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi m}} \]

Worked Example 6: Electron Wavelength (\(v = 1.00\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\))

\[ \lambda = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.00\times10^6)} = \boxed{7.27\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m} = 0.727\ \mathrm{nm}} \]

Worked Example 7: Minimum Velocity Uncertainty (\(\Delta x = 1.00\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}\))

\[ \Delta v_x \geq \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{4\pi(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.00\times10^{-10})} \implies \boxed{\Delta v_x \geq 5.79\times10^5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}} \]

4.7 Schrödinger Equation & 4.8 Atomic Orbitals

\[ \boxed{\hat{H}\psi = E\psi} \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi \qquad \int |\psi|^2\,d\tau = 1 \]
  • • Wavefunction (\(\psi\)): Mathematical function describing the quantum state.
  • • Probability density (\(|\psi|^2\)): Probability per unit volume of finding the electron near a point.
  • • Atomic Orbital: A one-electron wavefunction describing an electron state in an atom (not a fixed circular path).

4.11 Pauli Exclusion Principle & 4.12 Shell Capacity

Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in the same atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. Max 2 electrons per orbital with opposite spins: 1s [↑↓] Allowed vs 1s [↑↑] Not allowed.

Shell Capacity: Orbitals in shell \(n = n^2\). Max electrons = \(\boxed{2n^2}\) (K=2, L=8, M=18, N=32).

UNIT 05

ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATIONS

5.1–5.4 Aufbau, (n+l) Rule & Hund's Rule

Aufbau Principle: Electrons generally occupy lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s.

The \(n+l\) Rule: Lower \(n+l\) fills first; if equal, lower \(n\) fills first. For 4s (\(4+0=4\)) vs 3d (\(3+2=5\)), 4s fills before 3d.

Hund's Rule: Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing occurs (e.g., Nitrogen 2p):

Correct 2p:   [↑] [↑] [↑]
Incorrect 2p: [↑↓] [↑] [ ]

5.8 Paramagnetism vs Diamagnetism:
• Paramagnetic: Has ≥1 unpaired electrons; attracted to magnetic field (Fe³⁺, O atom, N atom).
• Diamagnetic: No unpaired electrons; weakly repelled (Zn²⁺).

UNIT 09 & 10

VALENCE BOND THEORY (VBT) & MOLECULAR ORBITAL THEORY (MOT)

Unit 09: Valence Bond Theory (σ & π Bonds)

VBT explains covalent bonding via overlap of atomic orbitals.
• Sigma (σ) bond: Electron density concentrated along the internuclear axis with cylindrical symmetry (s–s, s–p, end-on p–p, or hybrid overlap).
• Pi (π) bond: Sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals with electron density above and below the nodal internuclear axis. Restricted rotation around C=C double bonds occurs because rotating breaks sideways π overlap.

Unit 10: Molecular Orbital Theory (LCAO & Bond Order)

\[ \psi_+ = \psi_A + \psi_B\ (\text{Bonding }\sigma,\pi) \qquad \psi_- = \psi_A - \psi_B\ (\text{Antibonding }\sigma^*,\pi^*) \] \[ \boxed{\text{Bond Order (BO)} = \frac{N_b - N_a}{2}} \]

• B₂, C₂, N₂ ordering (2s–2p mixing): \(\sigma_{2s} < \sigma^*_{2s} < \pi_{2p} < \sigma_{2p} < \pi^*_{2p} < \sigma^*_{2p}\)
• O₂, F₂ ordering: \(\sigma_{2s} < \sigma^*_{2s} < \sigma_{2p} < \pi_{2p} < \pi^*_{2p} < \sigma^*_{2p}\)

Worked Example 11: Bond Order of \(\mathrm{O_2^+}\) & Paramagnetism of \(\mathrm{O_2}\)

• \(\mathrm{O_2}\) has 12 valence e⁻: \((\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma^*_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2p})^2(\pi_{2p})^4(\pi^*_{2p})^2 \implies BO = \frac{8-4}{2} = 2\). Two unpaired electrons in degenerate \(\pi^*_{2p}\) orbitals make O₂ paramagnetic!

• Removing one \(\pi^*\) electron gives \(\mathrm{O_2^+}\): \(N_b=8, N_a=3 \implies \boxed{BO = \frac{8-3}{2} = 2.5}\).

13.1–13.18 Periodic Size & Energy Trends

\[ \boxed{Z_{\mathrm{eff}} = Z - S} \qquad \text{Penetration: } s > p > d > f \qquad \boxed{\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S} \]

UNIT 15 & 17

OXIDATION–REDUCTION & BALANCING CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

15.1–15.15 Oxidation Numbers & Redox Types

• OIL RIG / LEO GER: Oxidation Is Loss of e⁻ (increase in ON); Reduction Is Gain of e⁻ (decrease in ON).
• Oxidizing Agent: Causes oxidation and is itself reduced (\(\mathrm{O_2, Cl_2, KMnO_4, K_2Cr_2O_7}\)).
• Reducing Agent: Causes reduction and is itself oxidized (\(\mathrm{Zn, Fe^{2+}, CO, H_2, I^-}\)).

Ex 16: Mn in \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)

\(+1 + x + 4(-2) = 0 \implies \boxed{x = +7}\)

Ex 17: Cr in \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) & S in \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)

\(2x + 7(-2) = -2 \implies \boxed{x = +6}\)
\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}: 2(+1)+x-8=0 \implies \boxed{+6}\)

• Disproportionation: Same element is both oxidized and reduced (\(\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2OH^- \rightarrow Cl^- + ClO^- + H_2O}\), Cl goes from 0 to −1 and +1).
• Comproportionation: Two oxidation states form an intermediate state (\(\mathrm{Fe + 2Fe^{3+} \rightarrow 3Fe^{2+}}\)).
• Non-Redox: Acid–base neutralization (\(\mathrm{HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O}\)) has no change in oxidation numbers.

20.6–20.8 Corrosion & Prevention

Iron oxidation: \(\mathrm{Fe \rightarrow Fe^{2+} + 2e^-}\); Oxygen reduction: \(\mathrm{O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^- \rightarrow 4OH^-}\). Prevented by coatings, galvanizing (Zn), sacrificial anodes (Zn/Mg), and cathodic protection.

20.12–20.16 Batteries, Potentiometry & Kinetics

Primary (non-rechargeable) vs Secondary (Lead-acid, Li-ion, NiMH). Positive \(E^\circ\) predicts thermodynamic spontaneity, NOT reaction rate (high activation energy or passivation can make reactions slow).
